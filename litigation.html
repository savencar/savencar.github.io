Litigation Tracker

Summary

This tracker monitors legal challenges, court filings, and judicial rulings surrounding federal policy changes, agency reorganizations, and the National Science Foundation’s (NSF) plans to restructure the National Center for Atmospheric Research (NCAR).

2026

  • August 20, 2026
    Environmental Defense Fund v. National Science Foundation

    The Environmental Defense Fund filed a lawsuit in the U.S. District Court for the District of Colorado challenging the Trump administration's unlawful efforts to dismantle climate research functions at the National Center for Atmospheric Research (NCAR) and close its headquarters at the historic Mesa Laboratory in Boulder. The legal action contends that the administration's abrupt, politically driven attempt to eliminate vital Earth system science programs and supercomputing and aviation assets—announced by White House budget director Russell Vought—violates the Administrative Procedure Act by lacking reasoned decision-making, ignoring statutory mandates, and failing to consider reliance interests or alternatives. Furthermore, the lawsuit asserts that the administration violated the National Historic Preservation Act by moving to close and potentially sell off the iconic, I.M. Pei-designed facility without initiating required public reviews or consultations with state and local preservation authorities.

    The Environmental Defense Fund is directly injured by the administration's decision to dismantle NCAR's climate research and alter the Mesa Laboratory, as it deprives the organization of essential climate data, models, supercomputing access, and research aircraft necessary for its mission, while forcing a costly diversion of resources and violating its members' aesthetic and procedural interests in the historic facility.

    Count One: Alleges that the defendants' decision to eliminate NCAR's climate research functions violates the Administrative Procedure Act because it is arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, lacking in reasoned explanation, motivated by political retaliation, and made without considering relevant data, reliance interests, or alternatives.

    Count Two: Alleges that the defendants violated the Administrative Procedure Act and the National Historic Preservation Act by failing to identify the I.M. Pei-designed NCAR Mesa Lab as a historic property, failing to consult with the State Historic Preservation Officer and the public, and unlawfully withholding required Section 106 review before taking actions to close or alter the facility.

    Updates:

    • Status: This case is ongoing.

  • March 16, 2026
    University Corporation for Atmospheric Research v. National Science Foundation

    The University Corporation for Atmospheric Research (UCAR) has filed a lawsuit against several federal agencies, including the National Science Foundation (NSF) and the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), alleging a coordinated campaign of unlawful retaliation against the State of Colorado. The complaint asserts that following Colorado's refusal to cede sovereign authority over election regulations and criminal justice—specifically regarding the prosecution of Tina Peters—the federal government began dismantling the National Center for Atmospheric Research (NCAR). Key retaliatory actions cited include the divestment of UCAR's stewardship of a major supercomputing center, the termination of multi-million-dollar research agreements, the imposition of burdensome reporting requirements, and the implementation of unconstitutional "gag orders" on UCAR and NCAR officials. UCAR seeks declaratory and injunctive relief to halt these actions, arguing they are arbitrary, capricious, and cause irreparable harm to American atmospheric research and national security.

    Count One: Alleges that the federal government violated the Administrative Procedure Act by taking arbitrary and pretextual adverse actions against UCAR and NCAR as part of an unconstitutional, coordinated policy of retaliation to punish the State of Colorado for exercising its sovereign constitutional powers.

    Count Two: Alleges that NSF's decision to transfer stewardship of the NCAR Wyoming Supercomputing Center (NWSC) violates the Administrative Procedure Act because the agency completely lacks the statutory or contractual authority to mandate such a transfer, and the decision itself is an arbitrary, unconstitutional act of political retaliation against Colorado.

    Count Three: Alleges that NOAA violated the Administrative Procedure Act by terminating UCAR's CAMP award without the legally required procedural safeguards, hearings, or performance-based justifications, relying instead on a pretextual shift in agency priorities to mask an unconstitutional, coordinated campaign of political retaliation.

    Count Four: Alleges that NSF violated the Administrative Procedure Act by imposing burdensome, ad hoc reporting requirements on UCAR without the legally mandated risk assessments or procedural notices, creating impossible compliance deadlines to deliberately manufacture a pretext for noncompliance.

    Count Five: Alleges that NSF violated the Administrative Procedure Act by imposing an unconstitutional gag order that severely chilled the First Amendment rights of UCAR and NCAR officials through a blanket prohibition on public comment regarding the agency's restructuring activities.

    Count Six: Alleges that NSF violated the Freedom of Information Act by failing to meet statutory deadlines, making pretextual demands to avoid searching for records, and unlawfully withholding public documents concerning the agency's actions against UCAR and NCAR, thereby entitling UCAR to injunctive relief and attorney fees.

    Count Seven: Alleges that NOAA violated the Freedom of Information Act by failing to meet statutory and extended deadlines, ignoring follow-up inquiries, and unlawfully withholding public records regarding NCAR after attempting to close the requests through "still interested" notices, entitling UCAR to injunctive relief and attorney fees.

    Count Eight: Alleges that OMB violated the Freedom of Information Act by failing to meet statutory deadlines, ignoring repeated inquiries, and unlawfully withholding public records regarding NCAR after issuing a single generic response claiming processing could take at least a year, entitling UCAR to injunctive relief and attorney fees.

    Updates:

    • May 7, 2026: The Union of Concerned Scientists published an analysis of internal administration documents released through discovery in the ongoing lawsuit, revealing that the OMB's fast-tracked plan to fragment NCAR was driven by political motives rather than scientific rationale.
    • June 1, 2026: Defendants are hereby PRELIMINARY ENJOINED from divesting UCAR or NCAR of any rights, resources, or responsibilities related to the NWSC.
    • August 26, 2026: UCAR expands its lawsuit to include claims against the NSF, NOAA, and OMB for failing to respond to public records requests under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). Key sections added in the amended complaint:
      • VIII. UCAR’s FOIA Requests and the Agencies’ FOIA Violations
      • IX. Chronology of Events
      • COUNT SIX, COUNT SEVEN, and COUNT EIGHT. Violation of the Freedom of Information Act

2025

  • May 28, 2025
    State of New York v. National Science Foundation

    In the lawsuit New York v. National Science Foundation, sixteen states challenged two major Trump Administration policies: the April 2025 "Priority Directive," which ceased long-standing efforts to promote STEM participation among underrepresented groups (women, minorities, and people with disabilities) in favor of a "colorblind" funding model, and the May 2025 "Indirect Cost Directive," which capped university overhead reimbursements at 15%. The plaintiff states alleged these actions violated the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) and the Constitution's Separation of Powers by ignoring congressional mandates and causing the mass termination of existing research grants. However, the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York denied a preliminary injunction in August 2025, ruling that it likely lacked jurisdiction under the Tucker Act because the states were essentially seeking monetary relief exceeding $10,000, a matter reserved for the Court of Federal Claims. Following this jurisdictional setback, the states voluntarily dismissed the case on August 22, 2025, effectively ending this specific district court challenge to the NSF's restructuring of scientific priorities and funding.

    Updates:

    • August 22, 2025: The plaintiffs filed a notice of voluntary dismissal.

  • May 5, 2025
    Association of American Universities v. National Science Foundation

    In the legal challenge Association of American Universities v. National Science Foundation, thirteen universities and three academic associations successfully overturned a May 2025 NSF policy that sought to impose a blanket 15% cap on "indirect cost" reimbursements for research grants, replacing the long-standing system of individually negotiated rates. The plaintiffs argued that the cap was "arbitrary and capricious" and lacked congressional authorization, as it would prevent institutions from recovering the actual infrastructure and administrative costs necessary to conduct federal research. In June 2025, U.S. District Judge Indira Talwani ruled in favor of the universities, finding that the NSF had ignored its own regulatory framework and failed to provide a reasoned explanation for the shift. The court subsequently vacated the policy and declared it unlawful; although the government initially appealed this decision to the First Circuit, it voluntarily dismissed that appeal in September 2025, leaving the court's rejection of the rate cap as the final ruling.

    Updates:

    • June 20, 2025: The court vacated Policy Notice 25-034, declaring the 15% rate cap unlawful, arbitrary and capricious, and contrary to law under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA).
    • September 26, 2025: The government filed a voluntary dismissal.

  • April 28, 2025
    American Federation of Government Employees v. Trump

    In the ongoing litigation of American Federation of Government Employees v. Trump, labor organizations and local governments are challenging Executive Order 14210 and its implementing "Accelerated Reduction-in-Force Procedures" (ARRPs) as unconstitutional attempts to dismantle federal agencies without congressional approval. While a district court initially halted these reorganizations through a preliminary injunction, the Supreme Court stayed that order in July 2025, suggesting the executive orders themselves were likely legal if implemented "consistent with applicable law." Despite this setback, the district court has allowed the case to proceed by compelling the government to produce internal ARRP documents over claims of executive privilege, a move recently upheld by the Ninth Circuit. As of February 2026, the legal battle has intensified with new challenges against specific personnel separations at FEMA, while the court continues to weigh whether the administration's large-scale staff reductions effectively override the statutory missions mandated by Congress.

    Updates:

    • Status: This case is ongoing.

2021

  • June 21, 2021
    United States v. Arthrex Inc.

    In United States v. Arthrex, Inc. (2021), the Supreme Court ruled that executive officers who exercise significant, unreviewable final authority on behalf of the federal government are "principal officers" under the Appointments Clause of the U.S. Constitution and must be nominated by the President and confirmed by the Senate. To resolve the constitutional defect where non-Senate-confirmed Administrative Patent Judges issued final administrative decisions, the Court held that the authority of such "inferior officers" must be subject to final review and executive oversight by a Senate-confirmed principal officer (in that case, the PTO Director).

    In the context of the NSF litigation, the Trump administration cited Arthrex to justify dismissing the National Science Board. Because the Presidential Appointment Efficiency and Streamlining Act of 2011 removed Senate confirmation for Board members, the White House argued that the unconfirmed NSB cannot constitutionally exercise binding authority over agency operations without oversight from a Senate-confirmed principal officer.

    Conversely, critics, lawmakers, and litigants challenge this legal rationale as a pretextual misapplication of patent tribunal precedent. They emphasize that the Board serves a distinct policy-setting and advisory role rather than an adjudicative function. Furthermore, they argue that subordinating the Board to the NSF Director directly violates the co-equal governance structure mandated by the National Science Foundation Act of 1950.
